# Consumer NZ's August 2026 electricity-reform claims are directionally supported by stream #256's evidence, but its "market dominance" and "60 reports" framing overstates precision the underlying data doesn't have

## Executive answer

- **Market dominance:** Consumer NZ's claim that the four gentailers "control most of the retail market and own substantial generation assets" is directionally correct but conflates two different, both real, concentration measures: ~92% of *generation* (2024) and ~84% of *retail* ICPs (most recent Electricity Authority figure found, end-2022) [OECD 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-surveys-new-zealand-2026_3ec5de98-en/full-report/towards-a-more-affordable-secure-and-sustainable-electricity-system_8422a0e1.html); [EA, retail gross margins](https://www.ea.govt.nz/news/general-news/new-zealands-electricity-retail-market-retail-gross-margins/). What it omits, and stream finding [gentailer-concentration-margins.md](../research/findings/other/gentailer-concentration-margins.md) flags directly, is that the EA's own retail-margin analysis found **no significant retail profit advantage for vertically-integrated gentailers over non-generating retailers** — so "dominance" (market-share/structural risk) and "excess profit from integration" (a margin claim) are not the same finding, and only the first is well evidenced [EA, retail gross margins](https://www.ea.govt.nz/news/general-news/new-zealands-electricity-retail-market-retail-gross-margins/).
- **Renewable investment:** Consumer NZ frames this as a general shortfall — "more investment in local power sources" — with no mention of storage duration. Stream finding [renewable-pipeline-hydro-marginal-pricing.md](../research/findings/other/renewable-pipeline-hydro-marginal-pricing.md) shows the committed/under-construction pipeline is >80% intermittent solar/wind and named batteries are ~2-hour duration, while the dry-year problem MBIE scopes at 3-5 TWh of *seasonal* storage. **Consumer NZ's ask, as reported, does not address the seasonal gap** — it would help average-year prices, not dry-year spikes, and the stream's own evidence is more precise than Consumer NZ's framing on this point.
- **"Over 60 reports... none from a consumer's perspective":** This specific characterisation does not hold up against at least one prominent, official, on-the-record counterexample: MBIE's 2018-2019 Electricity Price Review was explicitly commissioned to ask whether prices are "fair and equitable... to consumers" and is described by MBIE itself as unique for its "focus on the consumers' point of view" [MBIE, Electricity Price Review 2018-19](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/building-and-energy/energy-and-natural-resources/energy-consultations-and-reviews/electricity-price-2018-19). The "60 reports" *count* itself I could not verify or refute (I found no published list), so I treat the count as an unverified Consumer NZ estimate, but the "none... from a consumer's perspective" qualifier is contradicted by this one case, and independent commentary suggests the 2019 review's consumer-facing recommendations were only partially implemented, not that it wasn't consumer-framed [Consumer Advocacy Council on implementation](https://www.cac.org.nz/our-work/research/implementing-epr-recommendations).
- **Citable basis for the specific reform asks:** "More retail competition without breaking up gentailers" is already the object of live, dated regulatory action — the Electricity Authority's non-discrimination and hedge-access rules for independent retailers take effect from 1 July 2026, aimed at exactly the imbalance Consumer NZ describes [EA, level playing field](https://www.ea.govt.nz/news/press-release/electricity-authority-moves-to-level-the-power-playing-field/). The pricing-mechanism ask is answered by the companion issue (#929) and I do not re-answer it here. The renewable-investment ask has real evidence behind the *build-out* (see above) but not behind Consumer NZ's implicit claim that more of the same kind of investment closes the seasonal gap. The cross-party-strategy ask is a governance/process claim, not something with a citable factual verdict — I treat it as outside evidence-checking scope, consistent with this issue's political-neutrality guidance.
- Consumer NZ's own published statistics on its headline framing ("power bills up nearly twice the rate of inflation since the 1999 reform") are not fully consistent across its own outputs: a Consumer NZ campaign page gives a 177% nominal rise (~"nearly double" CPI) since 1999, while a separate Consumer NZ retrospective gives "at least 35% higher" in real terms since 1999 [Consumer NZ, 5 grim statistics](https://www.consumer.org.nz/consumer-rights-and-campaigns/5-grim-statistics-about-nz-power-prices); [Consumer NZ, Bradford reforms 25 years on](https://www.consumer.org.nz/home-and-living/home-energy/the-bradford-reforms-25-years-on). I could not independently verify either figure against a 27-year CPI series myself (out of scope for the two focused questions this issue asked me to answer), so I flag this as unverified rather than corroborated or refuted; it is not directly comparable to the stream's own 10-year finding below.

**Overall confidence:** Medium — the four researchable questions are each answered with at least one primary official source, and the two most load-bearing counter-claims (retail-margin parity; the 2019 review's consumer framing) have two independent sources each, but several of Consumer NZ's numbers (the "60 reports" count, the exact current retail-ICP split) rest on figures I could not independently verify to a primary dataset.

## Evidence

### What Consumer NZ actually claims

This finding is anchored on the RNZ article named in the issue, fetched directly (curl, HTTP 200, accessed 2026-07-30; the article's own byline reads "30 July 2026," a few days later than the "26 Jul 2026" date in the issue text — I cite what I actually fetched at that URL) [RNZ, 30 Jul 2026](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/business/843907/how-consumer-nz-wants-to-fix-the-electricity-market). Consumer NZ chief executive Jon Duffy is quoted identifying four problems and four matching asks:

1. Gentailer dominance — "Contact, Genesis, Mercury and Meridian control most of the retail market and own substantial generation assets, giving them advantages that smaller independent retailers and generators do not have" — addressed by *reducing* dominance and increasing competition, explicitly **not** breaking up the gentailers ("I was not suggesting the gentailers had to be broken up, but there needed to be more competition").
2. The marginal/merit-order pricing mechanism ("if 95 percent of our generation on a given day is generated by hydro, wind, and solar... but 5 percent is generated by burning gas and coal, that drives the price for all of the electricity generated that day") — addressed by revising the pricing mechanism. **This is the subject of companion issue #929 and I do not re-litigate it here.**
3. Insufficient homegrown renewable investment, leaving reliance on imported coal/gas — addressed by more local generation investment.
4. No cross-party energy strategy — addressed by a joint strategy prioritising affordability, security, and emissions.

The same article gives supporting statistics: household power bills have "risen at nearly twice the rate of wider inflation since the electricity market was reformed 27 years ago"; "more than 30,000" households were disconnected for non-payment in the past financial year; "25 percent of New Zealand households [are] struggling with their power bill"; and Duffy says "there's more than 60 reports that have been written on the electricity sector over the last couple of decades, and none of them was actually written from a consumer's perspective" [RNZ, 30 Jul 2026](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/business/843907/how-consumer-nz-wants-to-fix-the-electricity-market).

A slightly earlier Consumer NZ / 1News piece (9 June 2026) reports what reads as an earlier version of the same campaign, with one notable difference: it describes the market-structure ask as wanting to "**break up** the gentailers" by separating generation from retail — the opposite of Duffy's "I was not suggesting the gentailers had to be broken up" framing seven weeks later in the RNZ piece [1News/Consumer NZ, 9 Jun 2026](https://www.1news.co.nz/2026/06/09/what-needs-to-be-done-to-bring-down-power-prices-consumer-nz/). This June version also gives a market-share figure — "control about 90% of the electricity market" — without specifying generation or retail, and different household-hardship statistics (one in four struggling, one in ten took out a loan, one in five cut back power use). I flag this only to note that Consumer NZ's own framing of the "break-up" question moved between June and July 2026; I am not able to determine which statement is the organisation's current position, so I treat the RNZ (later, more specific) framing as primary for this finding, per the issue's own framing.

A separate, earlier Consumer NZ article critiquing the Electricity Authority's own reform package (undated on the page, but referencing "mid-2026" implementation, so likely first half of 2026) explicitly called for "structural separation between generation and retail operations" [Consumer NZ, EA plan critique](https://www.consumer.org.nz/home-and-living/home-energy/electricity-authoritys-plan-to-fix-market-is-too-little-too-late) — reinforcing that Consumer NZ's messaging on "break-up vs. more competition" has not been static across 2026, which matters for the issue's question of whether there is "a citable factual basis... for Consumer NZ's specific reform asks": the asks themselves have shifted, so I answer against the RNZ article's stated version only.

### Market dominance: two different concentration numbers, and a margin claim the regulator's own data doesn't support

Stream finding [gentailer-concentration-margins.md](../research/findings/other/gentailer-concentration-margins.md) already establishes, from the Electricity Authority and the OECD's 2026 New Zealand survey, that the four gentailers produced about 92% of 2024 generation, and that EA's 2021 structure-conduct-performance review found generation HHI had hovered around 2,000 since 2014 with Meridian gross-pivotal 90-95% of the time in 2019-June 2021 [OECD 2026, Table 2.6](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-surveys-new-zealand-2026_3ec5de98-en/full-report/towards-a-more-affordable-secure-and-sustainable-electricity-system_8422a0e1.html); [EA SCP information paper, 2021](https://www.ea.govt.nz/documents/6673/Information_paper_-_Review_of_structure_conduct_and_performance_-_Wholesale_el_vHrNwSE.pdf). That finding also lists an open follow-up question it did not answer: "What share of retail customers and ICPs is supplied by the four gentailers versus independent retailers?"

For this issue I looked for that number specifically, because Consumer NZ's claim is about the *retail* market ("control most of the retail market"), not generation. The most concrete official figure I could fetch directly is from the Electricity Authority's own article on retail gross margins: as of end-2022, "Genesis, Contact, Meridian, Mercury, and TrustPower each hold between 12-26% market share" while "the remaining 34 independent retailers collectively represent only 16% of the market" — i.e. the big incumbents held about 84% of the retail market at that point, with the article separately noting TrustPower's retail business was sold to Mercury in May 2022, lifting Mercury's share from 15% to 26% [EA, retail gross margins](https://www.ea.govt.nz/news/general-news/new-zealands-electricity-retail-market-retail-gross-margins/). I could not locate a more recent (2024-2026) primary retail-ICP-share breakdown — the EA's live "market share trends" dashboard (`emi.ea.govt.nz/r/5o4z1`) is an interactive tool that returned a server error on every fetch-ladder rung and could not be exported in this environment, so I do not have a verified current-year number and note this as a gap, not as evidence the 2022 figure still holds.

Critically, the same EA retail-margins article is direct evidence *against* one implicit reading of "market dominance" — that vertical integration itself inflates retail prices/profits. The Authority states there was "no noticeable pattern in the size of margins between the gentailers and the retailers without generation portfolios," with retail gross margins ranging $19.50-$56.57/MWh across all retailer types (mean $38.63/MWh), and concludes there is "no significant retail profit advantage for companies which also own generation assets" [EA, retail gross margins](https://www.ea.govt.nz/news/general-news/new-zealands-electricity-retail-market-retail-gross-margins/). This doesn't contradict the *structural* concentration finding (four firms controlling ~84-92% of the market is still a competition-policy concern on its own — it's why the EA/Commerce Commission stood up the Energy Competition Task Force in August 2024, and why non-discrimination and hedge-access rules take effect 1 July 2026 [EA, level playing field](https://www.ea.govt.nz/news/press-release/electricity-authority-moves-to-level-the-power-playing-field/)) — but it does mean the specific claim "gentailers use retail arms to extract extra margin from consumers" is not what the regulator's own retail-margin data shows. Consumer NZ's RNZ statement doesn't make that specific claim explicitly (it talks about barriers to new entrants, which the margin data doesn't speak to either way), but its wording — "control... and own substantial generation assets, giving them advantages" — invites the reader to infer a margin advantage that the EA's own analysis does not confirm. I rate this a fair-but-imprecise characterisation: the *structural* dominance claim is well evidenced; a *margin-advantage* reading of it is not.

### Renewable investment: Consumer NZ's ask is about average supply, not the seasonal storage gap the stream already identified

Stream finding [renewable-pipeline-hydro-marginal-pricing.md](../research/findings/other/renewable-pipeline-hydro-marginal-pricing.md) is unambiguous on this point and I do not repeat its sourcing in full here (see that finding's Sources list for the underlying EA/Transpower/MBIE citations). Its conclusion: the committed and under-construction generation pipeline is large (1,380 MW committed, 1,415 MW under construction as at Dec 2025) but ~82% intermittent (mostly solar), and the only publicly documented grid-scale batteries are ~2-hour duration (Meridian's Ruakākā, 100 MW/200 MWh; Genesis' planned Huntly BESS, 100 MW/200 MWh) — while MBIE's own briefing scopes the dry-year problem at approximately 3-5 TWh of seasonal storage or flexibility, roughly 10% of annual demand, a scale no named public project comes close to closing.

Consumer NZ's RNZ statement about renewables says only: "insufficient investment in homegrown energy" and, in Duffy's words, that "gentailers were now investing more in generation because of the growing demand from data centres. That's cold comfort for the 25 percent of New Zealand households struggling with their power bill" [RNZ, 30 Jul 2026](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/business/843907/how-consumer-nz-wants-to-fix-the-electricity-market). There is no mention of storage duration, seasonality, or the dry-year mechanism specifically — the ask, as reported, is simply "more renewable investment." Given the stream's own evidence, more solar/wind investment of the kind already in the pipeline would be expected to lower prices *in the periods it generates* (the merit-order effect) but would not, on current public evidence, close the dry-year gap that drove Meridian's 2026 request to access ~545 GWh of contingent Lake Pūkaki storage through winter 2028 [RNZ, Jul 2026, cited in the stream finding]. So: Consumer NZ's diagnosis (not enough renewable investment) is broadly consistent with the stream's evidence, but its remedy, as stated, doesn't distinguish between investment that helps average prices and investment that would actually close the seasonal risk driving dry-year spikes — a distinction the stream's own finding makes and Consumer NZ's summary does not.

### "Over 60 reports... none written from a consumer's perspective"

I could not find a published list of "60 reports" to check the count against, from either Consumer NZ's own materials or a search of MBIE/EA/Commerce Commission publication indexes; I treat the count itself as an unverified estimate from Duffy's interview, not a citable statistic, and say so rather than repeating it as fact.

The qualifier — "none of them was actually written from a consumer's perspective" — is a stronger, checkable claim, and I found one clear, official counterexample. MBIE's 2018-2019 Electricity Price Review page states directly that the review was commissioned in April 2018 to investigate whether the market "delivers... fair and equitable prices to consumers," and describes it as unique for "address[ing] the need for electricity prices to be fair and affordable, not just efficient or competitive," with a further "novel element" being "the review's focus on the consumers' point of view and their say in the direction of the sector" [MBIE, Electricity Price Review 2018-19](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/building-and-energy/energy-and-natural-resources/energy-consultations-and-reviews/electricity-price-2018-19). That review produced 32 recommendations in a May 2019 final report, including mandatory minimum standards for vulnerable consumers and bans on early-payment discounts and predatory "win-back" pricing that the review itself framed as favouring wealthier households over poorer ones [MBIE, Electricity Price Review 2018-19](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/building-and-energy/energy-and-natural-resources/energy-consultations-and-reviews/electricity-price-2018-19). As a second, independent source, the Consumer Advocacy Council — a body itself created partly out of that review's recommendations — tracks implementation of the review's recommendations without disputing that it was a consumer-focused review; its concern is that implementation has lagged, not that the review lacked a consumer lens [Consumer Advocacy Council, implementing EPR recommendations](https://www.cac.org.nz/our-work/research/implementing-epr-recommendations).

This doesn't mean Consumer NZ's broader point is wrong — a review being *framed* as consumer-focused is not the same as it *being run by* consumers or consumer advocates (the 2018-19 panel was ministerially appointed experts, not Consumer NZ or an equivalent body), and Consumer NZ's real complaint, read charitably, may be that no *previous report was itself written by a consumer advocacy organisation* rather than that none *considered* consumers. But the RNZ quote as published — "none of them was actually written from a consumer's perspective" — is not accurate as a literal claim against the 2019 review's own stated terms of reference, and I could not verify the "60 reports" count to check whether other candidate exceptions exist among the unnamed 60. I rate this claim Medium confidence *against* Consumer NZ's literal framing, specifically because I have one strong primary counterexample plus one independent corroborating source on that counterexample's consumer focus, but I have not reviewed all "60" reports (nor a list of them) to make a comprehensive count-level judgement.

### Is there a citable factual basis for the four specific reform asks?

- **Reduce gentailer dominance without breaking them up:** Yes, and this is already underway, not merely proposed. The Electricity Authority and Commerce Commission's joint Energy Competition Task Force, set up August 2024, led to draft Code amendments requiring the four gentailers not to give their own retail arms better wholesale-hedging terms than independent retailers, plus a review of standardised hedge products and futures-market transparency, with the non-discrimination obligations taking effect from 1 July 2026 [EA, level playing field](https://www.ea.govt.nz/news/press-release/electricity-authority-moves-to-level-the-power-playing-field/); [gentailer-concentration-margins.md](../research/findings/other/gentailer-concentration-margins.md). Consumer NZ itself has separately criticised these reforms as "too little too late," arguing for structural separation instead [Consumer NZ, EA plan critique](https://www.consumer.org.nz/home-and-living/home-energy/electricity-authoritys-plan-to-fix-market-is-too-little-too-late) — so the "more competition, no break-up" ask as stated in the RNZ article is, in effect, already regulatory policy in train; the live dispute is over whether that policy goes far enough, not whether the underlying problem is real.
- **Revise the pricing mechanism:** Out of scope for this finding by design — see companion issue #929, which was opened specifically to evaluate whether NZ's marginal/merit-order pricing explains the scale of recent price rises and what alternatives exist.
- **More renewable investment:** Real evidence supports *some* form of this (see above), but not Consumer NZ's undifferentiated framing of it as closing the affordability gap generally; the stream's own evidence says it helps average prices, not dry-year risk specifically.
- **Cross-party energy strategy:** This is a governance/process claim ("no comprehensive whole-of-system policy exists across parties") rather than a claim with an evidence-checkable factual verdict — whether a strategy would work depends on its content, which doesn't exist yet to evaluate. Per this issue's method note and the pending ADR-0025 political-neutrality charter (#907), I do not assess or compare individual parties' energy positions here; I note only that Duffy's framing is a process critique, not a specific policy claim I can verify against data.

### A note on the household-bill statistics, for context only

Two of the article's supporting statistics deserve a brief methodological flag, though verifying them precisely was outside this issue's four researcher questions:

- **"Nearly twice the rate of wider inflation since... reformed 27 years ago" (i.e. since 1999):** Consumer NZ's own separate materials give inconsistent versions of this same comparison — 177% nominal rise "nearly double" inflation on one campaign page, versus "at least 35% higher" in real terms on another [Consumer NZ, 5 grim statistics](https://www.consumer.org.nz/consumer-rights-and-campaigns/5-grim-statistics-about-nz-power-prices); [Consumer NZ, Bradford reforms 25 years on](https://www.consumer.org.nz/home-and-living/home-energy/the-bradford-reforms-25-years-on). These are not necessarily contradictory (a 177% nominal rise against, say, ~105% CPI inflation over 27 years would produce a real increase in that range), but I did not calculate a matching 1999-2026 CPI series myself to check the arithmetic, so I neither corroborate nor refute the "twice inflation" figure. Separately, stream finding [residential-electricity-real-price-trends.md](../research/findings/other/residential-electricity-real-price-trends.md) found only a modest 1.6-2.1% real (CPI-adjusted) increase over the most recent 10-year and 5-year windows (to March 2026) — a much smaller gap than "nearly twice inflation" would suggest, but that finding's window (2016-2026) is a different, shorter period than Consumer NZ's 27-year comparison, so the two are not directly comparable and I do not read this as a contradiction, only as a reminder that "since 1999" and "over the last decade" describe very different price paths in this sector.
- **"30,000+ disconnected... in the past financial year" / "25% struggling":** These are directionally consistent with, though not identical in method to, both the Electricity Authority's own administrative disconnection dashboard (which counted an average ~27,000 prepay disconnection *events* and ~810 postpay disconnections *per month* across Jan-Oct 2025, affecting about 10,000 unique prepay customers monthly) and Consumer NZ's own past surveys (40,000 households disconnected in the year to January 2024; one in five to one in four households struggling to pay in successive 2024-2026 surveys) — all cited in stream finding [energy-hardship-electricity-burden.md](../research/findings/other/energy-hardship-electricity-burden.md). I did not re-verify the specific "30,000" or "25%" figures against a primary dataset for this finding, since that stream finding already covers hardship measurement in more depth than this issue asked me to duplicate.

## What would change this conclusion

- Direct access to Consumer NZ's own "over 60 reports" list (or its full new report, which I could not locate as a standalone PDF/webpage separate from the RNZ and 1News coverage of it) would let me check the "none from a consumer's perspective" claim against every report, not just the one strong counterexample I found.
- A current (2024-2026) primary Electricity Authority retail-ICP or market-share breakdown — the live EMI "market share trends" dashboard could not be exported by any fetch-ladder rung in this environment — would confirm whether the ~84% end-2022 retail concentration figure still holds, and at what level.
- A published, government-commissioned review specifically authored *by* a consumer advocacy body (rather than an expert panel *focused on* consumers) would be a stronger test of Consumer NZ's literal "written from a consumer's perspective" claim; I could not find one, which is some indirect support for a narrower reading of Consumer NZ's point.
- A reproducible 1999-2026 CPI-adjusted electricity price series (I did not build one for this finding) would resolve whether "nearly twice the rate of inflation since 1999" is accurate, and would reconcile it with the stream's existing 10-year finding showing only a modest recent real increase.
- Access to Consumer NZ's full underlying report (not just press coverage of it) would let me check whether its renewable-investment ask actually does address seasonal/dry-year storage in more detail than the two press summaries I read — the two summaries I found (RNZ, 1News) may simply have compressed a more nuanced position.
- I could not verify the exact figure the RNZ article's "27 years" (i.e. 1999) reform date and "over 30,000" disconnections claims rest on directly against a primary MBIE/EA dataset within this issue's scope; a human or a follow-up research issue focused specifically on those two numbers would close that gap (see companion energy-hardship finding for the closest existing coverage).
